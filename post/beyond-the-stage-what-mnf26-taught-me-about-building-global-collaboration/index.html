Lessons in Community from MNF26

Cristobal Bozdogan · Oct 29, 2026

When people think about a successful conference, they usually think about what happens on stage.

The keynote speakers. The packed session rooms. The networking conversations. The announcements that make headlines.

But after spending the last year working on the Micronutrient Forum 2026 (MNF26), I've come to appreciate that the real story often happens behind the scenes.

And what a story it was.

MNF26 was a success by every major measure. Attendance was strong, sponsorship revenue exceeded expectations, exhibition space sold exceptionally well, and the scientific and industry programme delivered an impressive number of sessions and opportunities for collaboration. Internal forecasts projected the event to perform at a level comparable to previous editions despite operating in a more geographically constrained market and with significantly less exhibition inventory available.

What made this accomplishment particularly remarkable was the breadth of organisations involved.

Throughout the preparation, I had the opportunity to work with foundations, UN agencies, NGOs, research institutions, donors, universities, and private-sector partners. Organisations including CIFF, WHO, FAO, PATH, UNICEF, GAIN, CGIAR, Helen Keller International, World Vision, Rockefeller Foundation and many others all contributed in different ways to making the conference a success.

What attendees saw was four days of dialogue, learning and networking in Accra.

What they didn't see were the hundreds of conversations that made those four days possible.

The sponsor negotiations.

The grant agreements.

The reimbursement processes.

The invoicing discussions.

The speaker coordination.

The exhibition logistics.

The countless approvals, revisions, follow-ups and problem-solving exercises that happen quietly in the background.

One day might involve helping a partner navigate procurement requirements. Another could be finalising travel grant processes for delegates from multiple countries. The next could be resolving payment requirements for an international agency or ensuring a sponsor receives exactly the visibility and engagement opportunities they were promised.

None of this work is particularly visible.

Yet all of it matters.

MNF26 also reinforced something I've long believed: conferences are ultimately about trust.

Trust between organisers and sponsors.

Trust between donors and grantees.

Trust between researchers and policymakers.

Trust between organisations that may have different priorities, different operating models and different perspectives, but share a common commitment to improving global nutrition.

In a time when many international collaborations are facing budget pressures and growing uncertainty, it was encouraging to see so many organisations continue to invest in bringing people together.

Because at the end of the day, progress rarely happens in isolation.

It happens when people share ideas.

When partnerships are formed.

When evidence meets implementation.

When someone leaves a session with a new perspective or a new collaborator.

And when conversations started at a conference continue long after the event itself has ended.

As we move from delivery into reporting, follow-up and long-term partnership development, one lesson stands out above all others:

Great conferences are not defined solely by attendance numbers, sponsorship revenue, sold exhibition space or session counts.

Those metrics matter, and MNF26 performed strongly across all of them.

But the real success lies in the connections created between people and organisations working toward a common goal.

Those relationships are the true legacy of any congress.

Everything else is just the mechanism that helps bring them together.
