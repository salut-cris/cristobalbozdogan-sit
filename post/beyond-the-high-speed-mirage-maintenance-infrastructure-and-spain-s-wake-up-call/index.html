Beyond the High-Speed Mirage: Maintenance, Infrastructure, and Spain’s Wake-Up Call

Cristobal Bozdogan · Oct 30, 2026

295km/h on the Madrid to Valencia line on an Iryo high speed train.

Here I am in Valencia, reflecting on what feels like an unsettling trend. Every time I visit recently, there seems to be a major crisis or disruption unfolding. Two years ago, it was the catastrophic DANA flooding. Earlier this year, there was the high-speed train collision near Adamuz on the Madrid–Seville line. And now, another severe incident on the very same line.

Train disruptions are no longer isolated incidents in Spain; they are pointing to a structural problem. How can two major disasters occur on the same high-speed rail line in a single year? It is jarring, especially in a country that prides itself globally on having one of the world's premier high-speed rail (AVE) networks.

Construction Excellence vs. Maintenance Deficits

Politically, I have generally given credit where it felt due. Spain's macroeconomic performance over the past few years has been solid relative to European peers, and external shocks, from energy price volatility to the pandemic, were handled with reasonable resilience.

However, examining Spain’s infrastructure spending over the past decade reveals a troubling pattern. Capital expenditure has simply not kept pace with the needs of the network. Funding appears insufficient even for routine maintenance. Spain excels at building impressive mega-projects quickly and under budget, but it has repeatedly failed to allocate the long-term operational expenditure required to preserve them.

Perception, Risk, and Public Transit

This decay presents a severe image problem abroad. To be fair, infrastructure woes are hardly unique to Spain; Western Europe as a whole is grappling with deferred maintenance, system failures, and administrative paralysis. Yet, the drop in reliability stings when contrast against Spain’s modern identity.

Despite these incidents, rail remains statistically far safer than driving. Society has effectively normalized the extreme risks of personal vehicles:

  • Highway collisions occur daily with minimal national coverage.
  • Major train derailments or collisions command front-page headlines precisely because they are rare and catastrophic.

The psychological impact of rail incidents is far higher, making reliability and safety absolute prerequisites for maintaining public trust.

The Industrial Ripple Effects

The implications of these failures extend beyond national borders. Spanish rolling stock manufacturers and engineering consultancies like Talgo and CAF compete globally for multi-billion-euro contracts. When the domestic network experiences repeated critical failures, international clients naturally begin to scrutinize the ecosystem's integrity.

The train sets involved in recent incidents were manufactured by international giants like Siemens and Iryo’s Hitachi fleet—not domestic builders. In fact, right before this latest crash, Renfe signed a major procurement contract with Siemens.

Part of the reason Spanish operators turn to foreign manufacturers is that domestic acquisitions have faced severe hurdles. The Talgo Series 106 (Avlo/S-106) launch, for instance, was plagued by long development delays, severe vibration issues post-deployment, and an unpopular interior redesign that shifted from a standard 2+2 seating configuration to a cramped 3+2 layout. Add to that buggy headrest screens that illuminate at night, and the passenger experience suffered significantly, I can personally attest to that.

Broader Economic and Reputation Fallout

This marks a dark moment for Spain’s international standing. Over the past decade, the country successfully project an image of modernization, fiscal competence, and economic vitality. While domestic opinion on economic performance is always divided, the external narrative was overwhelmingly positive.

Systemic infrastructure failures risk undoing that progress. One incident can be written off as a tragic anomaly; two in short succession signal systemic neglect.

Tomorrow, I take an Iryo train back to Madrid, the same operator involved in the recent crash. The Valencia line utilizes modern ERTMS signaling systems, unlike the older security protocols installed on portions of the Seville line, so the technical risk profile is vastly different. Even so, the broader economic fallout of this second incident will be far greater than the first, forcing an urgent debate on how Spain funds, maintains, and safeguards its critical assets moving forward.
