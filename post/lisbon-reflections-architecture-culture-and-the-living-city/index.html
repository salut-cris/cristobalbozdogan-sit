Lisbon Reflections: Architecture, Culture, and the Living City

Cristobal Bozdogan · Nov 2, 2026

City street of Lisbon in Bairro Alto

I’ve been walking around Lisbon over the last few days, and what has struck me most is that it is a city that still feels very much alive.

That might sound like a strange thing to say about a European capital, but I think we are increasingly reaching a point where some of our most beautiful cities are becoming victims of their own success. Historic centres are polished, renovated and marketed until, little by little, they stop feeling like places where people actually live. They become places to visit rather than places to belong to.

Lisbon, despite the enormous amount of tourism it receives, somehow still seems to resist that.

There is obviously the Lisbon that everyone knows: the yellow trams, the tiled façades, the viewpoints, the steep streets and the constant glimpses of the Tagus. But behind all of that there is still a functioning city. People actually live in the centre. The cafés are not exclusively filled with tourists. You see people going to work, meeting friends, doing their shopping, sitting outside for a coffee or a beer. There is still that slightly chaotic rhythm of everyday life happening alongside the tourism.

Coming from Madrid, I found that especially interesting.

A City That Feels Genuinely Multicultural

Another thing that immediately stood out to me was just how multicultural Lisbon feels.

Compared with Madrid, there seems to be a much more visible integration of different communities into the everyday fabric of the city. Of course, you cannot separate this completely from Portugal’s colonial history, particularly its relationship with Africa, and that history is complicated and often very dark.

But walking through Lisbon today, you also see how different cultures have become part of what the city actually is.

It doesn’t feel like there is one historic Lisbon and then, separately, a multicultural Lisbon. The two exist on top of each other. Different languages, food, music and communities all occupy the same streets and public spaces. To me, that makes the city feel much more dynamic than a place simply preserving an image of its past.

There is something interesting about cultures that were historically connected through empire now actively participating in and reshaping the identity of the former imperial capital itself.

Avoiding the European “Disneyland” Effect

This was probably the part I kept thinking about the most.

Across Europe, and especially in Southern European cities, we talk constantly about overtourism. Barcelona is probably the most obvious example, but I think parts of Madrid are beginning to face similar questions.

What happens when the historic centre of a city becomes economically more valuable to visitors than to the people who actually live there?

Eventually you risk creating what I think of as the “Disneyland effect”: beautiful architecture, immaculate historic streets and endless restaurants and shops, but progressively less connection to the normal life of the city.

Lisbon certainly has many of the same pressures. You can see tourism everywhere. But what surprised me is that the centre still feels relatively mixed. Tourists, residents, old businesses, new restaurants, neighbourhood bars and international brands all coexist, sometimes very messily.

And maybe the messiness is actually important.

A city does not necessarily need to look perfectly curated to be successful. Sometimes the opposite is true. Lisbon feels interesting precisely because it hasn't been completely homogenised.

Architecture Without Anxiety

Architecturally, Lisbon was also not quite what I expected.

Madrid’s centre is undeniably beautiful, but a lot of its visual identity is anchored in a particular period, especially the bourgeois architecture of the late 19th and early 20th centuries. There is a consistency to it that gives Madrid much of its elegance.

Lisbon feels much less concerned with that kind of consistency.

You can walk past a historic tiled house, then an Art Deco building, then something Brutalist, then a completely contemporary office block. Some newer buildings make an effort to disappear into their surroundings. Others do almost the opposite and seem completely comfortable making a modern statement next to much older architecture.

Normally this kind of mixture should feel chaotic. Somehow, in Lisbon, it largely works.

Part of that is probably the geography. The city is built across steep hills and constantly changing levels, so buildings appear stacked above one another rather than organised along a completely uniform skyline. The terrain itself almost gives permission for the architecture to be irregular.

And then underneath everything are the black-and-white Portuguese cobblestones, beautiful, iconic and absolutely determined to make you regret your choice of shoes the moment it rains.

That continuity at street level seems to hold everything together.

A City That Hasn't Finished Becoming Itself

Maybe that is ultimately what I liked most about Lisbon.

It doesn't feel finished.

And I mean that as a compliment.

There are cities that seem increasingly concerned with preserving a particular version of themselves. Lisbon certainly protects its history, but it also seems comfortable allowing new layers to sit beside the old ones.

The result isn't always perfect. Some buildings are beautifully restored while others are visibly deteriorating. Expensive new developments sit beside much older neighbourhoods. Tourism, international investment and local life are clearly competing for the same space.

But cities have always been contradictory.

Perhaps the real danger isn't contradiction, but removing it. Once a city becomes too polished, too consistent or too optimised for the people passing through it, something important disappears.

Lisbon still has that friction.

And that brings me, eventually, to why I'm here.

I'm in Lisbon ahead of the International Union of Microbiological Societies congress, helping prepare for a few very busy days bringing together people from different countries, institutions and parts of the scientific community.

After spending some time walking around the city before getting properly into congress mode, there is something quite fitting about holding an international congress here.

Congresses are obviously about science, meetings, collaboration and exchanging knowledge. But there is another element that I sometimes think we underestimate when we work on them every day: for a few days, thousands of people with completely different backgrounds arrive in the same place and temporarily create their own little international community.

Lisbon already seems to understand that idea quite well.

Different histories, cultures and architectural periods don't just sit next to each other here. They overlap, clash occasionally, adapt and eventually become part of the same city.

Not a bad setting for an international congress.
